Working-economy tracker

Main Street Barometer

The stock market measures the wealth of people who own stocks — about the richest tenth of the country. This tracker follows a different set of vital signs: what's happening to wages, debt, and basic-needs affordability for the other nine-tenths.

📅 Built September 19, 2026 🔁 Update cadence varies by source — quarterly (Fed, NY Fed), monthly (BLS, BEA), annual (USDA)
01 — Labor & wages

Is work paying?

What a paycheck actually buys, and how many people are cashing one.

Real wage growth, year over year
−0.3%
● Falling behind
Nominal wages rose 3.1% but consumer prices rose 3.4% (Aug 2025–Aug 2026) — the recovery in purchasing power that ran through early 2026 has now stalled.
Source: BLS, Real Earnings Summary, Aug 2026
Labor force participation rate
61.6%
▼ Lowest since 1976*
Down 0.9 points in just six months (Dec 2025–Jun 2026). *Excluding the 2020 pandemic disruption — the sharpest non-recession drop in decades.
Source: Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, Aug 2026
Labor share of nonfarm business income
52.8%
▼ Lowest ever recorded
The lowest reading since this BLS series began in 1947. Labor share held near 60–66% for decades post-WWII before a sustained decline starting in the early 2000s.
Source: BLS, "Labor share at its lowest level," Q2 2026
CEO-to-typical-worker pay ratio
325:1
▲ Up from 21:1 in 1965
Typical worker pay has grown 26% since 1965; CEO pay has grown over 1,000%. See chart below.
Source: Economic Policy Institute, "CEO Pay in 2025," Sept 2026

02 — Household debt & fragility

What's the cushion look like?

Savings, revolving debt, and how many households are already behind.

Personal savings rate
2.7%
● Thin cushion
Share of disposable income households are setting aside — well below the ~7% long-run pre-2020 norm.
Source: BEA, Personal Income & Outlays, Jun 2026
Credit card balances, 90+ days delinquent
12.8%
▲ Highest since the Great Recession
Up from 7.6% in Q3 2022. About 60% of the 175 million U.S. cardholders carry a revolving balance month to month.
Source: NY Fed, Liberty Street Economics, Aug 2026
Auto loan delinquency
5.5%
▲ Series record
Share of auto loan balances seriously delinquent, Q2 2026 — the highest since this data series began.
Source: NY Fed, Household Debt & Credit Report, Q2 2026
Total household debt
$18.8T
● 4.7% in some stage of delinquency
Credit card debt alone: $1.26 trillion, nearing the all-time high set in late 2025.
Source: NY Fed, Household Debt & Credit Report, Q2 2026
Adults using "buy now, pay later" for groceries
29%
▲ Up from 14% two years ago
Roughly 1 in 3 consumers have used apps like Klarna or Affirm to finance groceries — usage has roughly doubled in two years, a marker of cash-flow stress, not credit-access.
Source: LendingTree survey, 6,000+ U.S. consumers, 2026
03 — Wealth & income distribution

Who's actually capturing the growth?

Where the wealth sits, and how concentrated the spending power has become.

Top 1% share of household net worth
31.6%
▲ Up from 22.8% in 1989
The top 1% alone now holds nearly a third of all U.S. household wealth.
Source: Federal Reserve, Distributional Financial Accounts, Q1 2026
Bottom 50% share of household net worth
2.5%
▼ Down from 3.5% in 1989
Half the country splits about one-fortieth of the nation's wealth.
Source: Federal Reserve, Distributional Financial Accounts, Q1 2026
Bottom 90% share of total consumer spending
~50%
● Record concentration
The top 10% of earners now account for nearly half of all U.S. consumer spending — the highest share since this data began in 1989 — leaving the other 90% the remaining half.
Source: Moody's Analytics (Mark Zandi), Q2 2025
Top 10%'s share of discretionary spending
36.2%
● Nearly matches the bottom 70% combined
On non-essentials alone (excludes housing, healthcare, groceries), the top 10% of earners spend almost as much in total dollars as the bottom 70% of earners combined. The bottom 10% account for just 2.1% of discretionary spending.
Source: Bank of America Institute (Krisberg & Tinsley), 2023 data, trend confirmed continuing into 2026
Top 10% share of household net worth
67.9%
▲ Up from 60.8% in 1989
The top decile now owns more than two-thirds of everything.
Source: Federal Reserve, Distributional Financial Accounts, Q1 2026

04 — Basic-needs affordability

Can people afford to live?

Housing, food, and healthcare — the non-negotiables.

Workers who can't afford the median-priced home
~74%
● Roughly 3 in 4
Independent studies in 2026 put the range at 65–77% of households priced out of the median home, depending on methodology.
Source: NAHB / Bankrate housing-affordability studies, 2026 (range cited)
U.S. households facing food insecurity
13.7%
▲ Trending toward 16% in late 2025
18.3 million households, 2024 — USDA's last annual report before discontinuing the survey. Monthly tracking since shows the rate climbing further, hitting 16% in November 2025.
Source: USDA ERS (2024 annual); Purdue Center for Food Demand Analysis (monthly tracker)
Adults who delayed or skipped care over cost
92%
● Nearly universal
Adults under 28 were hit hardest — over 94% postponed treatment for financial reasons. Source is an industry survey (a debt-settlement firm) rather than a government or peer-reviewed one, though the direction is corroborated by separate Gallup polling on health-cost anxiety.
Source: JG Wentworth survey, 1,500+ U.S. adults, Aug 2026 — directionally corroborated by Gallup
05 — The order things get given up

What goes first, and what people hold onto longest

People don't lose everything at once. Utilities go into arrears first; rent gets severely stretched next; the car — the last link to a paycheck — is held onto until it's physically repossessed.

Renters severely cost-burdened (50%+ of income on rent)
26%
▲ Record high
12.1 million renter households. A broader 49% (22.7 million households) pay more than the standard 30% affordability threshold — also a record.
Source: Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies, "America's Rental Housing 2026"
Households behind on utility bills
~1 in 6
● $23B in arrears nationally
About 4 million households faced a utility disconnection in 2025. Often the first bill to slide — held less tightly than rent or the car payment.
Source: National Energy Assistance Directors Association (NEADA), Jan 2026
Vehicles actually repossessed
1.73M
▲ Up 43% since 2022
Highest annual repossession count since 2009. This is the line your auto-delinquency figure (above) is heading toward — the point where the last avenue to work is physically taken away.
Source: Cox Automotive, 2024 data (most recent annual figure)
06 — Why the car-loan trap is structural

People aren't just falling behind — the loans are rigged to make that likely

Lenders have stretched loan terms to keep monthly payments looking affordable, which quietly extends how long a buyer stays underwater — explaining why delinquency keeps climbing even as people fight hardest to keep paying.

New-vehicle buyers financing for 84+ months
23.9%
▲ Record share
Nearly 1 in 4 new-car buyers now take 7-year loans. Average loan term across all new-vehicle purchases: 70.4 months, also a record.
Source: Edmunds, Q2 2026 financing report
New-vehicle trade-ins with negative equity
28.1%
▲ 4-year high
Averaging $6,905 still owed on the old loan — an all-time record. That debt gets rolled into the new loan, so the buyer starts underwater on day one.
Source: Edmunds, Q3 2025 data (most recent published breakdown)
07 — Slack the headline numbers hide

The unemployment rate everyone quotes isn't the whole picture

A low headline rate can coexist with people working part-time who need full-time work, people who've stopped looking, and people juggling two jobs just to get by.

Prime-age (25–54) male employment rate
85.8%
▼ Down from 96.0% in 1953
If prime-age men worked today at the 1953 rate, about 10 million more would be employed. A cleaner "who's actually working" gauge than the labor-force participation rate, since it excludes students and retirees by design.
Source: BLS, employment-population ratio, Aug 2026
Workers holding more than one job
5.2%
● 8.5 million people
A wage-inadequacy signal the unemployment rate can't see: people who are "employed" but still can't get by on one paycheck.
Source: BLS, Employment Situation, Aug 2026

The real slack is nearly double the headline rate

August 2026 — U-3 (official) vs. U-6 (includes underemployed & marginally attached)
Source: BLS / Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis (FRED), Aug 2026
08 — The safety net people fall back on before they fall through

What absorbs the shock when a paycheck doesn't stretch

Before someone hits the metrics above, they typically move back in with family or raid their own future — quietly, before it ever shows up as delinquency or default.

Adults 18–29 living with their parents
49%
▲ Up 12 points since 2019
Family absorbing a shock that used to be absorbed by independent living — a form of hidden housing insecurity that doesn't show up in eviction or homelessness data.
Source: Federal Reserve, Survey of Household Economics and Decisionmaking (SHED), 2025 data
401(k) participants taking hardship withdrawals
6%
▲ Record share
Top reasons: avoiding foreclosure or eviction (36%), medical expenses (31%), tuition (13%). People are liquidating retirement security to cover today's bills — a quiet leading indicator of tomorrow's crisis.
Source: Vanguard, "How America Saves 2026"
Adults who couldn't cover a $400 emergency expense in cash
37%
● More than 1 in 3
The classic liquidity-buffer canary: distinct from net worth, since someone can own a home and a 401(k) on paper and still be one flat tire away from a payday loan.
Source: Federal Reserve, Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households, 2025 data (pub. May 2026)
